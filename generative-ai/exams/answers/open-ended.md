# <center> **Preguntas a Desarrollar**
---

1. The ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 standard provides a unified approach for the processes and products involved in requirements engineering throughout the life cycle of systems and software. It specifies required processes, guidelines for applying these processes, and defines the required information items and their content, ensuring conformance with standards like ISO/IEC 15288 and 12207.

2. Tailored conformance in ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 refers to the ability to modify or select specific clauses from the standard to fit the specific needs of a project, particularly when the full set of requirements may not be necessary. It allows for adjustments in the application of processes and information items while still maintaining a form of conformance.

3. The consensus development process in IEEE standards ensures that a broad range of stakeholders, including volunteers from different fields and interests, collaborate to create standards that are fair and representative of multiple viewpoints. This process promotes inclusivity and fairness, allowing for standards to be more widely accepted and effective.
 
4. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 acknowledges that the implementation of its standards may require the use of patented technology. However, the standard does not take responsibility for identifying patent rights or ensuring the validity of any patents. It places the responsibility on users to verify and manage patent rights and the risk of infringement themselves.
    
5. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 specifies the content requirements for information items produced during the requirements engineering processes. These items must conform to the guidelines and requirements outlined in Clause 7, Clause 9, and Annex A of the standard. This ensures that all necessary information is accurately captured and documented throughout the system life cycle.
    
6. 'Shall' is used to indicate mandatory binding provisions in requirement statements, making it clear that the provision must be met. The term 'must' is often avoided due to potential misinterpretation, as it may not be legally enforceable.

7. A well-formed requirement must be verifiable, necessary, unambiguous, consistent, complete, singular, feasible, traceable, and bounded by constraints. It must define the performance of the system without imposing unnecessary design constraints.

8. Requirements attributes help in managing and understanding requirements. Examples include identification (unique number or tag), stakeholder priority (indicating importance), risk (grading in terms of potential impact), and source (origin of the requirement).

9. Stakeholder intentions, which are initially informal and often incomplete, are analyzed, defined, and refined through requirements engineering processes. Tools like the Concept of Operations aid in evolving these intentions into structured, formal requirements that are validated against real-world needs.

10. Common pitfalls include using vague or subjective terms (e.g., 'user-friendly'), superlatives (e.g., 'best'), ambiguous pronouns, and open-ended, non-verifiable terms (e.g., 'if possible'). Requirements should avoid negative statements and be written clearly to prevent misinterpretation.

11. Traceability ensures that stakeholder requirements are linked to the stakeholders' needs, allowing for validation of operational services against these requirements. This fosters accountability and clarity throughout the project lifecycle.

12. Stakeholder analysis helps identify the stakeholders involved with the system, their needs, expectations, and desires, which are then transformed into a coherent set of stakeholder requirements for the system.

13. The Stakeholder Requirements Definition Process generates a baseline set of requirements that flow into the architectural design process. In this phase, requirements are allocated, decomposed, and traced to system elements, ensuring that design aligns with stakeholder needs.

14. While both standards address requirements engineering, ISO/IEC 15288 focuses on system life cycle processes, while ISO/IEC 12207 emphasizes software life cycle processes. There may also be minor differences in terminology and emphasis on specific activities.

15. Specification of required system characteristics: This ensures clarity on what the system must deliver, aiding in meeting user expectations. Definition of constraints on a system solution: Identifying constraints helps set boundaries for design and implementation, reducing risks associated with scope creep and feasibility issues.

16. Stakeholder Requirements Specification (StRS), System Requirements Specification (SyRS), and Software Requirements Specification (SRS).

17. The StRS describes the organization's motivation for developing or changing a system, defines processes and policies for its use, and documents top-level requirements from the stakeholder perspective, including the needs of users, operators, and maintainers.

18. The SyRS identifies the technical specifications for the selected system, defines high-level system requirements from the domain perspective, and describes system interactions with its external environment.

19. Multiple specification documents can provide different views or perspectives on the same product, making it easier to address the specific needs of various stakeholders and ensuring comprehensive coverage of requirements.

20. MDD approaches maintain system information within a modeling tool, allowing for contextual storage, easy access, and the ability to generate reports or tables from the model, thus ensuring that the required information is well-organized and readily available.
